Data and updates

Series come from official sources: INDEC's data through the Argentine government's time-series API (datos.gob.ar), and the BCRA's statistics API and published tables. The exceptions are the IPC Congreso used in the long-run CPI (see below) and three hand-kept files for net reserves (see Net reserves), each row with its source. An automated job fetches every series twice a day, checks it and publishes the site.

Each download is checked before it is accepted: no missing months or quarters, no dates moving backwards, no published observations disappearing, no non-positive index values, and rates between 0 and 100. A series that fails keeps its last good data and its chart shows a notice. Large revisions and unusually large moves are accepted but logged for review.

Sources revise their data, sometimes years back. Every value the site has ever received is stored with the date it was received, so any chart can be rebuilt as it looked on a past date. The full revision histories are in the project repository.

Seasonally adjusted series are INDEC's. Where INDEC publishes only the original series (for example, EMAE by sector), the site shows changes that compare like periods (year over year, accumulated) and does not show month-on-month changes, which would mostly reflect seasonality.

Changes and averages

m/m, q/q
Change from the previous month or quarter: Xt / Xt−1 − 1.
y/y
Change from the same month or quarter a year earlier.
YTD (year to date)
Change since December of the previous year. In December it equals the December-to-December change.
Accumulated y/y
INDEC's variación acumulada: the sum (equivalently, the average) of the months published so far in the year, over the same months a year earlier. For GDP this is annual growth once all four quarters are in.
Rates
Changes in a rate (capacity utilization, investment rate) are differences in percentage points, not percent changes.
Rebasing
Any index can be rebased so that a chosen month (for example, the month before a presidency starts) equals 100. Rebasing changes levels only, never percent changes.
Carryover
The statistical carry-over (arrastre estadístico): next year's growth if activity stayed for the whole year at the level of this year's last month or quarter (seasonally adjusted). For the year in progress, the table shows this year's growth if the remaining periods stayed at the latest level.

Presidencies

Charts can be colored and summarized by presidential term. A term's first period is the first full month (or quarter) after the inauguration; the month of a handover belongs to the outgoing president, since the incoming one has had no time to change anything. Milei, inaugurated on 10 December 2023, starts in January 2024 (index base December 2023 = 100), and December 2023 counts for Alberto Fernández. Between Fernando de la Rúa's resignation on 20 December 2001 and Eduardo Duhalde's inauguration on 2 January 2002, three interim presidents served for a few days each; December 2001 stays with De la Rúa and Duhalde's term starts in January 2002.

Term statistics use each full term, whatever time range a chart shows:

  • Average of m/m or q/q changes: geometric, (∏(1 + rt))1/n − 1. This is the constant rate that compounds to the term's cumulative change; an arithmetic mean of compounding rates overstates it.
  • y/y views: inflation or growth annualized over the term, (Xend / Xbase)12/m − 1, where m is the number of months from the base month to the latest month. An average of overlapping y/y rates is not the term's inflation.
  • Levels: start (base month), latest or final value, change, average, minimum and maximum.

Party colors: Peronism (PJ, Frente para la Victoria, Frente de Todos) light blue; UCR and the Alianza red; PRO and Cambiemos gold; La Libertad Avanza purple.

Episodes

Some charts can mark dated shocks ("Episodes"), with a short neutral description of each. With episodes switched on, the presidency table also shows the average monthly rate without the marked months, so readers can see how much a few months drive a term's average. Episodes are off by default; which months to exclude is a judgment the reader can inspect and switch off. The current list:

  1. Post-primary devaluation (Aug–Sep 2023): the official exchange rate was raised about 22% the day after the August 2023 primaries.
  2. Plan Platita and the December devaluation (Nov–Dec 2023): pre-election fiscal and monetary expansion; the official exchange rate then more than doubled on 13 December 2023.
  3. Correction of relative prices (Jan–Apr 2024): pass-through of the December devaluation and the adjustment of regulated prices.

Weights and incidence

EMAE sector weights are each sector's share of 2004 GDP at constant prices, the base year of the index, from INDEC's quarterly value added by industry. Net taxes on products are the remainder. With these weights the sector changes add up to the change in total EMAE.

CPI weights are recovered from the published indices. A fixed-base (Laspeyres-type) index with every component on the same base satisfies It = Σi wi Ii,t in every month, so regressing the headline index on its components without a constant returns the weights. The recovered weights reproduce the published headline index within 0.05% (categories) and 0.22% (divisions) since December 2016. They are the weights at December 2016 prices.

Incidence is a component's contribution to the change in the total, in percentage points:

incidencek = wk (Xk,t − Xk,b) / Σj wj Xj,b × 100

where b is the comparison period: the previous month (m/m), the same month a year earlier (y/y) or the previous December (YTD). For the accumulated change the same formula is applied to year-to-date sums. Incidences add up to the total's change.

GDP

Contributions to growth use the original (not seasonally adjusted) series at constant 2004 prices, which are additive. The contribution of a component C to y/y GDP growth is (Ct − Ct−4) / GDPt−4 × 100. A residual group (inventories and statistical discrepancy on the demand side; other services and net taxes on the supply side; other assets in investment) closes the gap to total growth exactly.

Real GDP since 1993 chain-links INDEC's accounts at 1993 prices (quarterly growth through the first quarter of 2004) with the accounts at 2004 prices (from the second quarter of 2004). Growth rates are kept from each base; the level is that of the 2004 base. INDEC's 2004 rebasing raised the measured level of GDP and changed growth in the overlapping years; the splice does not revise the 1993–2003 figures.

Quarter tracker: the seasonally adjusted EMAE averaged over each quarter, next to seasonally adjusted quarterly GDP. INDEC reconciles the two, so for published quarters their q/q changes coincide (within 0.03 percentage points since 2004). For the quarter in progress the tracker uses the months published so far: their average against the previous quarter's (q/q), what the quarter would show if the remaining months stayed at the latest month's level, and the y/y change of the same months in the original series.

Investment rate: gross fixed capital formation over GDP, both at current prices, summed over the last four quarters to remove seasonality.

CPI since 1943

From January 2007, INDEC was intervened by the national government, under Secretary of Domestic Trade Guillermo Moreno, and its CPI understated inflation. In February 2013 the IMF issued a declaration of censure against Argentina over the quality of its CPI and GDP data (IMF Press Release 13/33). After the change of government, Decree 55/2016 declared an administrative emergency of the national statistical system in January 2016 (InfoLEG), and INDEC suspended the CPI while it was rebuilt. A new CPI for Greater Buenos Aires started with May 2016 and the national CPI with December 2016; the IMF removed the censure in November 2016 (IMF Press Release 16/497).

The chart shows two series, chain-linked by monthly changes:

  • Official (as published): INDEC's historical CPI to December 2006; CPI-GBA (2007–2013); the national urban IPC-NU (January 2014 – October 2015, the last figure published before the change of government); CPI-GBA base April 2016 (May–December 2016); and the national CPI from January 2017. There is no official CPI for November 2015 – April 2016.
  • Corrected: the same, with the IPC Congreso, a composite of private inflation estimates, for January 2007 – April 2016, including the November 2015 – April 2016 gap. While INDEC was intervened, members of Congress published the average of private estimates and kept the private sources confidential, to shield them from the fines and lawsuits the Secretariat of Domestic Trade brought against private estimators.

Both series are equal through December 2006, so in levels the distance between them is the inflation the official figures left out: from January 2007 to October 2015, official CPI inflation was 159.6% and corrected inflation 631.8%, so the corrected price level ends 2.8 times the official one. Across the November 2015 – April 2016 gap, the official level carries on with the corrected series' changes so that later levels stay comparable, but the gap months are left blank and no change spanning them is shown.

CPI with the 2017/18 basket

The national CPI weights each of its twelve divisions by spending shares from the 2004/05 household expenditure survey (INDEC, CPI methodology). INDEC prepared a new index with weights from the 2017/18 survey, due to start with January 2026 data; its introduction was postponed. The estimate applies the new division weights to the published division indices from December 2025:

CFt = Ht × Σi wnewi ri,t / Σi woldi ri,t,   ri,t = Ii,t / Ii,Dec 2025

H is the official headline index; wold are the current basket's weights (recovered as above) at December 2025 prices; wnew are the 2017/18 division weights, taken as applying in December 2025. The denominator rebuilds the official index from the same division data, so the gap between the two series is the effect of reweighting alone (the rebuilt index matches the official one within 0.02%). The estimate captures only the shift in weights across divisions; the new index would also change the items and weights within divisions and by region, which cannot be reproduced from published data.

Money & Banking

Data come from the BCRA's statistics API, daily. Exchange rates, interest rates and reserves are shown daily. For daily data, a month-on-month or year-on-year change compares with the last business day on or before the same date a month or a year earlier.

Monthly averages, not end of month. Money stocks (base money, aggregates, deposits, loans) are shown as monthly averages of daily data by default, as in the BCRA's own monetary reports, for three reasons. Month-end figures swing with the payments calendar (wages paid around the turn of the month, the June and December bonus, holidays). Banks manage their reserve requirements on monthly averages, so the average is the figure that reflects their behavior. And the CPI and activity data measure a whole month, so real money balances should compare the month's average money with the month's average prices; with month-end stocks, the timing mismatch is half a month of inflation, which in 2023 and early 2024 was several percentage points. End-of-month stocks are right where flows have to add up to a change in a stock, as in the sources of base money, and base money can also be shown at month end.

Aggregates. M1 = currency held by the public + current accounts; M2 = M1 + savings accounts; M3 = currency + all peso deposits of the public and private non-financial sectors; private M2 uses private deposits only. All in pesos.

Sources of base money. The BCRA attributes each day's change in base money to its operations (factores de explicación). Each month's sum of each group is shown in percentage points of base money at the end of the previous month, so the bars add up to the month's change in end-of-month base money.

Real terms. Peso amounts can be shown deflated by the CPI, in pesos of the latest month with CPI data. For 2007–2016 the deflator is the corrected CPI (see CPI since 1943), since deflating by the official figures would overstate real growth in those years.

BCRA balance sheet. From the BCRA's historical table "Balance del Banco Central de la República Argentina", monthly since 1940, in its analytical presentation (sources of base money and absorption), grouped and valued for monetary analysis. Each month is converted from the currency of its time (peso moneda nacional, peso ley, peso argentino, austral) to current pesos with the BCRA's own factors. Changes in the valuation of the Treasury's non-transferable bills (below) and the May 2026 change in the accounting of repos create breaks in some lines.

Weekly balance sheet table. The BCRA's weekly summary balance of assets and liabilities (balances on the 7th, 15th, 23rd and last day of each month, since 2002), line by line, in the BCRA's own wording and grouping for each year; pesos of each date, dollars at each balance's exchange rate, or shares of total assets. When two dates are compared, lines are matched by name, so a line renamed or regrouped between the two dates shows no change. Where the BCRA's file has a blank or an error cell, the table shows a dash.

Valuation of the Treasury's non-transferable bills (Letras Intransferibles). The BCRA has received these dollar bills from the Treasury since January 2006 in exchange for reserves. Their book value depends on the valuation criterion in force, which has changed three times:

  • Technical value (face value plus accrued interest), from 2006 to the weekly balance of 15 July 2018.
  • Estimated present value (Board Resolution 209/18, 5 July 2018, applied from the 2017 financial statements): cash flows discounted at the yields of comparable Argentine-law bonds, from 23 July 2018 to 23 December 2019. The switch cut their book value from about USD 49 bn to 37 bn; after the August 2019 primaries it fell to about USD 10 bn. During 2019 the BCRA also reported net equity with the bills at technical value.
  • Technical value again from 31 December 2019 (Law 27,541, art. 62; Board Resolution 2/2020), on the grounds that the BCRA would hold them to maturity; the reversal added about $2.1 trillion pesos.
  • Amortized cost, capped at recoverable value (Board Resolution 128/24, recorded in the 2023 financial statements and in the weekly balance from 7 May 2024): their book value fell from about USD 69 bn to 18 bn. In April 2025 the Treasury repurchased bills with a face value of about USD 16.9 bn using IMF funds (Decree 179/25).

Sources: BCRA financial statements for 2018, 2019, 2023 and 2025; BCRA, "El BCRA adecua el valor contable de las Letras Intransferibles"; dates of each change as they appear in the weekly balances.

Inflation expectations. The median 12-month-ahead expectation in the BCRA's Survey of Market Expectations (REM), dated by the month of the survey, against CPI inflation over the following 12 months and over the previous 12 months.

Net reserves

Net reserves are estimates, not official statistics. Gross reserves include dollars the BCRA owes or does not control: banks' reserve requirements on dollar deposits, the swap with the People's Bank of China, repos, loans from international agencies. "Net reserves" subtract some of these, but there is no official or universal definition. Analysts, the BCRA and the IMF subtract different liabilities, so the same balance sheet gives different numbers. The site shows three conventions and, for the latest balance, a table with each line of each one.

  • Standard (market): the estimate most private analysts publish: gross reserves minus the BCRA's foreign-currency liabilities that sit inside them, and minus the Treasury's dollar deposits, which belong to the Treasury.
  • Liquid: the standard estimate minus gold, which cannot be used for payments without being sold. SDR holdings cannot be separated in the weekly balance, so they are not deducted.
  • IMF-style: an approximation of net international reserves as defined for the 2025 Extended Fund Facility (Technical Memorandum of Understanding, May 2026 staff report, Attachment II, ¶11): gross reserves minus swaps, reserve requirements, deposit insurance (SEDESA), non-resident deposits, foreign-currency liabilities with an original maturity of one year or less, and the Fund's cumulative net purchases since the program started. The Treasury's dollar deposits are not deducted. The weekly balance does not show deposit insurance, BOPREAL maturing within a year, forwards or the US Treasury swap, so this line runs above the IMF's published figures: −0.5 against −2.4 bn at end-2024, and −9.2 against −11.8 bn at end-2025. The IMF also values non-dollar items at fixed program exchange rates; the site uses current rates.

Data. The BCRA's weekly summary balance sheet (balances on the 7th, 15th, 23rd and last day of each month, since 2003), converted to dollars at each balance's exchange rate: gross reserves, banks' current accounts in foreign currency, obligations with international agencies, gold. The Treasury's dollar deposits come from the monthly balance sheet (the latest month-end in force at each weekly date). The China swap is valued with the BCRA's own yuan quote. Month-end balances are lower than mid-month ones because banks move their dollar reserve requirements around the turn of the month.

Hand-kept files. Three items are not in any machine-readable source; they are kept in the repository with a source for every row (data/manual):

  • China swap booked in reserves (CNY bn): CNY 70 bn from October 2014, credited in tranches; CNY 130 bn since the CNY 60 bn expansion was credited on 17 December 2018, renewed since (most recently in August 2026, for five years). The BCRA announced the tranches only through January 2015; the build-up from February to September 2015 is read from the BCRA's weekly balance, where the swap was recorded with repo liabilities (net of peso repos). That path matches the announced tranches and settles at CNY 70 bn. The activated tranche (CNY 35 bn since 2023) is part of the CNY 130 bn, not in addition to it.
  • BCRA repos with foreign banks (USD m outstanding): January–November 2016, and from January 2025. The 2017–2019 repos were contracted by the Treasury, not the BCRA, and enter through Treasury deposits. Since 2025 all of the BCRA's repos have had original maturities above one year, so the IMF-style line does not deduct them. BIS loans are in the weekly balance's obligations with international agencies.
  • IMF purchases under the 2025 program (USD m), net of repurchases.

The weekly balance's repo-liability line cannot replace the repo file: before July 2024 it mixes in peso repos and, through 2022, the China swap, and since then it carries other items of USD 1–3 bn. The pipeline flags any move in that line of more than USD 500 m that the repo file does not explain, so the file is updated when the BCRA contracts or repays a repo.
